# atendimento_sac_tickets

A customer-service (SAC) export: `clientes` (700), `tickets` (1400) and `mensagens` (about 3500
messages on those tickets). Generated by `atendimento_sac_tickets` in the commerce pack;
`preview.json` shows the first rows of each table.

- **Long operator prose.** `tickets.descricao` opens with the customer's full name and the
  complaint, then carries whatever the operator typed in: the customer's CPF (masked or digits),
  their phone, their email, and on one ticket in five a relative's name, CPF and phone (people
  who appear in no other column). It ends with the resolution.
- **Denormalised customer fields.** As support exports usually are, each ticket repeats the
  customer's name and email (`nome_cliente`, `email_cliente`), which lets a check ask that no
  word of the row's own name or email survives in its description.
- **The false-positive trap.** `tickets.protocolo` is an eleven-digit protocol number, the year
  followed by a sequence (`20240012345`), and every one fails the CPF check-digit rule. It is
  shaped like a CPF and must not be treated as one: not classified CPF, not regenerated into a
  valid CPF, and, as wanted here, not touched at all. Messages quote it back
  ("Meu protocolo é 20240012345").
- **Messages** alternate customer and agent; customers paste their CPF or phone, agents greet the
  customer by first name and repeat the protocol.
- **Agents**: 24 people, named on each ticket in `atendente`.

What the vocabulary cannot yet say, kept here as prose:

- A protocol number quoted inside a message should survive verbatim, while the CPF next to it
  must not. A "this substring survives" assertion (the inverse of `no_source_survival`, scoped
  to values from another column) would pin it.
- A relative's name inside a description lives in no source column, so its survival can only be
  checked for digits (its CPF and phone); a check for names found by the corpus inside free text
  would reach it.
